# Literature Review

## A. Data Compression Fundamentals

#### 1) Principles of Data Compression

Data compression is the process of encoding information in a form that reduces the storage space or transmission bandwidth required. The information can be reduced due to the presence of redundancy, which is the portion of the data with statistical structure rather than unique information. In real-world signals, redundancy commonly arises from strong correlations between neighbouring samples, repeating patterns, and constrained amplitude dynamics.

This redundancy can only be exploited up to the entropy of its source, as established by Shannon [10]. This implies that sources exhibiting low entropy can be represented using fewer bits per symbol, while sources with mostly unique symbols allow for little compressibility. By identifying and exploiting these statistical dependencies within data, redundancies can be removed to approach the entropy limit, reducing storage and transmission bandwidth requirements.

#### 2) Evaluation Metrics

Since compression algorithms vary in how they exploit redundancy and in the trade-offs they employ between data reduction and signal fidelity, standardized metrics are required to evaluate their performance. Compression is typically assessed by one or more of the following: compression ratio (CR); rate-distortion metrics such as percentage root-mean-square difference (PRD), mean squared error (MSE), or peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR); similarity indices such as normalized cross-correlation (NCC) or the structural similarity index measure (SSIM); and composite quality scores (QS). Additional metrics such as bitrate and latency are also important in applications like audio, video, and real-time signal compression.

CR measures data reduction as a ratio of original bits to compressed bits, with higher values indicating more efficient compression. Rate-distortion metrics quantify the error between the reconstructed and original data, where lower values denote higher fidelity. QS gives a composite measure of compression efficiency against reconstruction quality, and similarity indices such as NCC evaluate morphological similarity, with values close to 1 indicating strong similarity. Healthcare monitoring systems typically focus on CR and PRD, since together they quantify the trade-off between data reduction and reconstruction accuracy that is central to diagnostic accuracy under bandwidth and timing constraints.

However, CR, PRD, MSE, PSNR, NCC, SSIM, and QS are all computed directly on signal samples and carry no explicit information about *which* parts of the ECG waveform were distorted, even though not all distortion is equally important diagnostically: a compressor that removes high-frequency noise can *raise* PRD while making the signal easier to diagnose, and a compressor that distorts the ST segment can produce a deceptively low PRD. A family of *diagnostic* distortion measures has been developed specifically to address this gap. Němcová et al. [27] provide a comprehensive review of this family, cataloguing more than 40 objective ECG quality methods and grouping them into three categories: methods without diagnostic information (e.g., PRD and its normalized variants, MSE, SNR); methods with diagnostic information derived from the wavelet transform (e.g., Wavelet-based Weighted PRD (WWPRD) and Wavelet-Energy-based Diagnostic Distortion (WEDD)); and methods with diagnostic information derived from ECG delineation (e.g., Weighted Diagnostic Distortion (WDD), which weights the distortion of 18 clinically extracted features such as P-wave duration, QT interval, and ST elevation).

Němcová et al. tested 16 non-diagnostic methods and the full set of wavelet-based diagnostic methods on the CSE database (125 fifteen-lead ECG records) compressed with a wavelet-transform-and-SPIHT algorithm at 33 different bit rates, using two cardiologists' manual classification of the reconstructed signals into three quality groups (perfect, good, non-evaluable) as ground truth. On the basis of five criteria — rate-distortion trend, sensitivity/variability at the clinically important boundary of 0.8 bits per sample, cross-correlation/cluster analysis, computational demand, and citation popularity — they recommend using a combination of eight methods together (PSim SDNN, QS, SNR1, MSE, PRDN1, MAX, STDERR, and WEDD computed via the stationary wavelet transform) rather than relying on any single metric. Their strictest threshold for "perfect quality" reconstruction was PRDN1 < 5.4% and WEDD < 3.8% at the 0.8 bps boundary, stricter than the ≈9% PRDN threshold commonly cited elsewhere in the literature. They found WDD to have the highest theoretical diagnostic content of any method tested, but do not recommend it in practice: it is extremely computationally demanding, and its accuracy is bounded by that of the delineation algorithm used to extract its 18 underlying features, none of which is perfectly reliable, particularly for pathological signals. The authors explicitly caution that their numeric thresholds are validated only for the specific compressor (WT+SPIHT) and database (CSE) tested and should be treated as an approximate guide rather than universal constants for other compressors or datasets.

This body of work indicates that evaluating an ECG compression system on PRD/CR alone risks missing cases where numerical fidelity is preserved while diagnostically important segments are distorted, or vice versa. A supplementary diagnostic metric such as WEDD, or at minimum a multi-metric evaluation along the lines recommended by Němcová et al., would give a more complete picture of a compression algorithm's suitability for clinical use — a consideration this work returns to when defining its own evaluation methodology.

#### 3) Classification of Compression Techniques

Compression algorithms generally comprise two components: a model, which analyzes the input data to detect patterns and redundancy, and a coder, which exploits the redundancy found to generate efficient code, assigning shorter codewords to high-probability values and longer codewords to low-probability values. Most compression algorithms share similar coder logic — Huffman, Lempel-Ziv-Welch (LZW), Golomb-Rice, or arithmetic coding — and primarily differ in their model component [4]. Three broad model-component techniques distinguish different classes of algorithm, and ECG compression algorithms can be categorized accordingly into time-domain, transform-based, and parameter-extraction techniques.

Time-domain techniques, also known as direct techniques, work on the raw ECG samples. They exploit redundancy present in the data without transformation into another domain, typically using prediction or interpolation. These methods are usually lossy and have low computational complexity, making them common in real-time applications on low-power hardware. Representative approaches include amplitude zone time epoch coding (AZTEC), capable of high CR at the expense of a PRD as large as 28%; coordinate reduction time encoding system (CORTES), with a low CR of 2:1 but high fidelity; the turning point method, which combines AZTEC and CORTES for a CR of 4.8 and PRD of 7%; and predictive encoding methods such as differential pulse code modulation (DPCM), which achieve CR from 8 to 23 with PRD from 1.7% to 2.8% [11].

Transform-domain techniques, in contrast, convert ECG signals into an alternative domain — frequency (Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform (DCT)), wavelet (wavelet transform (WT)), or a learned domain — before compression. Used in both lossy and lossless algorithms, this approach allows a higher compression ratio than direct techniques and is comparatively insensitive to noise. After transformation, the coefficients can be thresholded or quantized to reduce data volume before entropy coding, which can achieve CR as high as 28:1 but may increase distortion of the reconstructed signal.

Parameter extraction techniques compress only key features — the width, amplitude, duration, and intervals of cardiac components — which are then used to reconstruct the signal. Algorithms in this category are usually lossy and include neural-based or syntactic methods, peak picking, and linear prediction methods [12].

A fourth paradigm that does not fit neatly into this three-way taxonomy is **compressed sensing (CS)**, which merges acquisition and compression into a single step rather than compressing an already-fully-sampled signal. Prasasti et al. [28] provide a systematic mapping study and systematic literature review of CS applied to biomedical signals (ECG, EEG, PPG, and SCG) in wireless body sensor networks. Their review screened 622 articles (2014–2025) across Scopus, ScienceDirect, and IEEE Xplore following the PRISMA protocol, and a VOSviewer bibliometric analysis of the resulting corpus found "compressed sensing" (and its variant "compressive sensing") to be the single most prominent keyword in the field by occurrence count (168) and total link strength (195), closely clustered with "deep learning" and "ECG." On performance, the review reports that dynamic CS for multi-lead ECG has demonstrated compression ratios of up to 16 without degrading signal metrics, but at the cost of high computational complexity, which the authors describe as a bottleneck for wearable-device integration. The review also discusses an adaptive CS technique the authors call Compressive Adaptive Sense and Search (CASS), reported to achieve near-optimal performance at lower signal-to-noise ratios, but whose dependence on costly adaptive-sensing hardware is said to restrict scalability. Prasasti et al.'s own stated research gap calls for low-complexity, hardware-accelerated CS reconstruction algorithms to minimize computational overhead and power in real-time wireless body sensor network deployments, and for cost-effective adaptive-sensing hardware architectures offering dynamic measurement schemes without a significant complexity or power penalty.

This stated gap — adaptive, hardware-efficient, real-time CS — is conceptually adjacent to, but not the same as, the gap this work targets. Prasasti et al. describe a gap within the CS sub-field specifically (adaptive *sensing matrices* and measurement schemes), not a gap in switching between different compression *paradigms* (e.g., time-domain vs. transform vs. CS) at runtime based on signal morphology. Reference [28] is therefore cited here as evidence that CS is a large, active paradigm not otherwise covered by the three-way taxonomy above, rather than as direct evidence that this work's specific gap is unaddressed.

## B. Compression of ECG Signals

#### 1) ECG Signal Characteristics

An electrocardiogram signal records electrical impulses generated by the mechanical activity of the heart. It captures critical phases, as labelled in Fig. 1(a), including atrial and ventricular activation, ventricular depolarization (QRS complex), and repolarization (T wave). The QRS complex represents the most diagnostically significant component, typically ranging from 0.06 to 0.10 s; its abnormal prolongation beyond 0.10–0.12 s, illustrated in Fig. 1(b), may indicate a block in the normal conduction pathways through the ventricles [13].

The majority of ECG signal energy lies within the 0.05–100 Hz band; however, diagnostically relevant information can also be present beyond this range, in subtle morphological features such as notches and slurs at higher frequencies. This wide spectral distribution, together with repetitive waveform structure, presents unique challenges for compression algorithms, which must preserve both gross morphology and fine temporal detail.

#### 2) Survey of ECG Compression Algorithms

ECG compression algorithms are designed to exploit the high predictability and repetitive morphology of cardiac signals to reduce data volume while preserving clinically meaningful information. This subsection reviews representative ECG compression algorithms, illustrating how different forms of redundancy in the waveform are exploited and providing a basis for understanding the capabilities and limitations of existing methods. The reviewed algorithms are commonly evaluated on benchmark datasets such as the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia database (360 Hz, 11-bit resolution) and the PTB Diagnostic ECG Database (1 kHz, 10–16-bit resolution), reflecting practical clinical and monitoring conditions.

Among several compression algorithms and techniques, most systems employ a variant of predictive encoding followed by entropy coding (Huffman or Golomb-Rice) due to their low computational complexity. A hardware-efficient compression system based on predictive coding and entropy encoding has been demonstrated in [9]. This time-domain algorithm first reduces sample amplitudes by first-order differencing, then performs packet-wise mean calculation for noise immunity; the data is then divided into low, medium, and high amplitude bands and entropy-coded with Golomb-Rice, followed by run-length encoding. The scheme is strictly lossless (PRD = 0), achieves an average CR of 2.89, and is lightweight enough for low-power wearable devices operating in real time.

Another approach combines a fuzzy logic-based predictor with a region-based Huffman entropy coder [14]. The fuzzy decision table parameters were optimized offline via particle swarm optimization (PSO), and lossless entropy coding designed for hardware simplicity followed, achieving an average CR of 2.84 on MIT-BIH. While these lossless algorithms guarantee perfect signal reconstruction and simple hardware implementation, their limited compression ratios may prove insufficient during extended monitoring periods, motivating exploration of lossy techniques capable of higher compression at the cost of approximation.

A transform-domain, lossy compression system combining wavelet decomposition with bio-inspired optimization has been developed in [15]. The Haar Wavelet Transform (HWT) decomposes ECG signals into blocks, extracting spectral and temporal information simultaneously via computationally efficient matrix multiplication. The COVIDOA (Coronavirus Disease Optimization Algorithm), a metaheuristic operating through four phases mimicking a viral lifecycle, selects the optimal subset of wavelet coefficients to minimize PRD for a desired compression ratio. This block-based Haar approach achieves a CR of 32 with a PRD of approximately 0.25% and an NCC of 0.95, while reducing processing time relative to many wavelet-based methods; however, its PRD degrades during high-activity segments such as premature beats, since the fixed-size coefficient subset cannot capture rapid morphological changes.

A lossy time-domain algorithm that fundamentally differs from these paradigms is presented in [16]: a deep-learning approach comprising a Binary Convolutional Auto-Encoder (BCAE), which learns an end-to-end binary representation of individual heartbeats, and a Residual Error Compensation (REC) network, implemented as a shallow multi-layer perceptron, which refines reconstruction by minimizing residual errors. This method achieves a CR of 117.33 with a PRD of 7.76% and QS of 18.75, requiring roughly 400 training epochs and achieving an inference time of 0.0101 s per heartbeat on a Raspberry Pi. Its high training cost and potential generalization limitations for unseen cardiac morphologies without retraining complicate deployment across diverse patient populations.

In contrast to lossless algorithms, lossy methods demonstrate impressive compression ratios but exhibit sensitivity to morphological variability and increased computational complexity. This has motivated exploration of adaptive compression at the acquisition or parameter level. A real-time adaptive prediction-based compression algorithm with entropy encoding is proposed in [17], selecting the most suitable linear predictor — zero, first, or second order — depending on the ECG region (initial, QRS, flat, transition before QRS), followed by a modified variable-length coder designed for low-power transmission, achieving a CR of 2.67 on MIT-BIH. This regional adaptivity reduces the prediction residual and improves lossless coding efficiency, but the adaptation is limited to local prediction selection rather than a change of the global compression algorithm.

Romdhane et al. [18] instead explore adaptive compression at the acquisition stage. Unlike most algorithmic compressors, their proposed level-crossing analog-to-digital converter performs data reduction during acquisition, sampling only when predefined amplitude thresholds are crossed. The system achieves a clinically acceptable PRD of 9% on the PTB database and, at moderately high resolutions, a CR of up to 5:1. This demonstrates adaptivity at the acquisition stage, though compression behaviour is not optimized with respect to diagnostic content.

A hybrid ECG compression system is presented in [19]: the signal is first processed with adaptive thresholding to identify R-peaks, from which Q- and S-waves are detected; the region of interest and the region outside it are then compressed separately, with lossless DPCM applied to the region of interest and lossy DWT elsewhere. Implemented in MATLAB and evaluated on MIT-BIH, this hybrid, region-based approach achieves a PRD of 2.81 and CR of 15.6:1 — notably strong performance for a scheme that still applies lossless coding where diagnostic content is concentrated.

Overall, prior work demonstrates substantial progress in ECG compression, as summarized in Table I. Region-based approaches in particular show strong potential for balancing compression ratio and signal fidelity by allocating lossless or near-lossless coding to diagnostically significant segments and more aggressive lossy coding elsewhere. However, [19], the strongest-performing region-based scheme reviewed here, was evaluated in an offline (MATLAB, software) environment with no consideration of real-time or hardware deployment constraints. This motivates the investigation, taken up in Section C below, of whether such region-adaptive strategies can be implemented directly in hardware under real-time constraints.

## C. Hardware Acceleration for Biosignal Processing

#### 1) Motivation for Hardware Acceleration

Software implementations of ECG signal compression on microcontrollers or general-purpose processors are feasible, but typically cannot simultaneously meet the high compression ratios and low-latency, real-time requirements of medical monitoring systems [23]. These platforms incur substantial overhead from instruction execution, shared memory access, and sequential processing; as algorithmic complexity increases, this overhead limits achievable throughput and makes it difficult to guarantee minimal processing delay over long-term monitoring. Among the alternatives — GPU, FPGA, and ASIC accelerators — each offers a different balance of flexibility, throughput, and power efficiency, with FPGAs in particular occupying a middle ground of reconfigurability and energy efficiency between general-purpose processors and fixed-function ASICs [8].

Hardware accelerators enable compression pipelines to be tailored at the datapath level, mapping the model and coder stages to dedicated, pipelined, and parallel processing blocks [24]. This reduces reliance on sequential instruction execution and shared functional units, allowing samples to flow through fixed-latency processing stages, which significantly reduces control and scheduling overhead relative to software systems and improves resource utilization, idle-cycle reduction, and sustained throughput [25]. For adaptive compression specifically, the reconfigurable nature of FPGAs is particularly valuable, since it allows switching between compression modes or parameters while maintaining bounded-latency streaming. Although MCUs can, in principle, support adaptive compression, their concurrent processing demands make it difficult to guarantee sustained throughput under real-time conditions.

#### 2) Software and Embedded Implementations

Elgendi et al. [20] developed a lossy algorithm on a personal computer (Intel i5, 2.2 GHz); through decimation, it achieved a CR of 4.5 with a low PRD of 0.53% and, owing to its low time complexity, a total processing time of 0.029 s. These results, however, were obtained in a PC-based environment that does not replicate the resource constraints of real-time, wearable monitoring. Microcontroller implementations have also been proposed: on an Arduino Uno, the RAKE algorithm [21] performs predictive coding and entropy encoding using arithmetic operations rather than lookup tables and floating-point computation, achieving a CR of 2.14 with a processing latency of 127 μs per sample at a 4 MHz clock. A low-complexity lossy algorithm using hybrid pulse-width-modulation-integrated quantization [22] achieves a CR of 4 and a PRD of 0.33% using only 9 arithmetic operations per sample, with a processing latency of 0.046–1.337 μs per sample on an ESP32 microcontroller (10–240 MHz clock). Such microcontroller implementations demonstrate that cumulative latency scales with sampling rate and signal dimensionality under sequential processing; while they address resource constraints, they generally show lower compression ratios, illustrating the trade-off between compression ratio and latency in real-time health-monitoring systems.

#### 3) ASIC Implementations

A third hardware option reported in the literature, alongside PC and microcontroller implementations, is the fully custom application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC). Deepu et al. [29] present an ECG recording system-on-chip (SoC) with an on-chip lossless compressor for wearable/ambulatory sensors, illustrating the power and area achievable when the compressor is folded into a custom analog-and-digital SoC rather than run as software on a general-purpose or microcontroller core. The compression scheme uses a second-order linear slope predictor, chosen after the authors empirically compared 1st- through 4th-order predictors on MIT-BIH and found the second order gave the lowest mean absolute and root-mean-square prediction error overall, even though higher orders performed better locally within the high-amplitude-variation QRS segment. The resulting prediction error is packed into a fixed 16-bit output using a custom priority-encoded, five-frame-type data-packaging scheme rather than Huffman or arithmetic coding, which the authors note would require large on-chip lookup memory to remain lossless — a design choice justified on the basis that memory-based entropy coders would consume more chip area than the compression savings provide in an ultra-low-power ASIC context. Implemented in a 0.35 μm CMOS process, the four-channel compressor occupies 0.4 mm² and consumes 535 nW/channel at 2.4 V for a 512 Hz sampling rate, achieving an average lossless CR of 2.25 on MIT-BIH (2.198 on the separate MIT-BIH Compression Test Database) and 2.55 measured on-chip under varying simulated heart-rate conditions. The design also implements periodic "resynchronization frames" — original, uncoded samples sent every 4 s — so that a single wireless packet loss does not permanently desynchronize the decompressor, a practical real-time-transmission detail not discussed elsewhere in this review. Against two other published lossless ECG compressors, the authors report roughly 23× fewer gates than a Golomb-coding-based design for a 5.8% lower CR, and roughly 6.4× fewer gates than a selective-Huffman VLSI design for an 8% lower CR, while consuming 535 nW/channel versus 36.4 μW/channel for the selective-Huffman comparator — roughly a 68× reduction. Relative to the FPGA implementations discussed in §C.4, this ASIC achieves substantially lower power per channel, but its compression scheme is fixed in silicon at fabrication time and cannot be reconfigured post-deployment to switch prediction order, coding scheme, or compression paradigm at runtime — a trade-off (ASIC: lowest power, fixed function; FPGA: higher power, runtime-reconfigurable) that motivates the choice of FPGA over ASIC as the target platform for the adaptive system proposed in this work.

A second ASIC worth noting is that of Tseng, Chen, and Lu [30], who fabricated a chip that *adaptively switches between two different compression algorithms* within a single ECG beat rather than applying one algorithm uniformly. Motivated by the observation that, after CS reconstruction, most of the error between the original and reconstructed signal concentrates in the QRS region, and that this error grows further as the compression ratio increases, the authors compress the region around the QRS complex using a near-lossless predictive/Huffman scheme they call NPC (near-precise compressed), and compress the remainder of the R–R interval using compressed sensing. The split point is fixed in advance, per beat, by dividing the R–R interval into a CS-compressed length and an NPC-compressed length using R–R timing information; a control unit then switches multiplexers to route each region to the appropriate algorithm. The measurement matrix used for CS is a low-cost binary block-diagonal design requiring only adders rather than multipliers, chosen specifically to reduce hardware cost. The authors report that this adaptive integration yields "notable improvements" in both SNR and CR relative to applying either algorithm alone, evaluated on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database. The design was fabricated in TSMC 0.18 μm CMOS, operating at 60 MHz with a gate count of only 2.69 K, and was additionally implemented on a Xilinx Kintex-7 FPGA — described by the authors as using low hardware resources — to demonstrate the design's compression performance outside the fabricated chip.

This is, to our knowledge, the closest existing hardware implementation to the region-adaptive, multi-algorithm switching this work investigates, and it materially narrows — though does not close — the gap motivating this work; see the revised discussion in §D.

#### 4) FPGA Implementations

FPGAs have been increasingly used for ECG compression owing to their support for parallel processing, reconfigurability, and high real-time throughput. A nearly lossless compression algorithm combining linear adaptive prediction, run-length encoding, and Golomb-Rice coding has reportedly been implemented on a Xilinx Virtex-5 [7]; operating in a pipelined mode, the design is reported to achieve continuous real-time operation at one ECG sample per clock cycle, consuming a total of 0.08 W.  The Golomb-Rice compressor reviewed in [9] similarly exemplifies hardware-friendly compression design, consuming only 408 LUTs and 51 flip-flops on an Artix-7 FPGA with 7.2 μs latency and a total power of 2.9 W, dominated by I/O power (2.7 W). Chowdhury and Cheung [26] implemented an FFT-based compressor on a ZedBoard (Xilinx Zynq-7000 SoC), utilizing 75 DSPs, 5,765 LUTs, and 7,031 registers per compression unit; the same design is reported to achieve a maximum compression rate of 90% (i.e., a CR of roughly 10:1) while keeping normalized PRD below 9%, reported by the authors as 5 percentage points higher than the state-of-the-art hardware implementation available to them at the time. More recently, Reddy and Tallapragada [31] report a two-stage lossless Integer Adaptive Predictor (IAP) compressor implemented on FPGA, preceded by an FNLMS-based adaptive denoising filter designed with a hybrid systolic-folding structure to reduce the filter's own power, delay, and area overhead; evaluated on MIT-BIH and PTB using Xilinx tools and MATLAB, the authors report a CR of 45.23% on MIT-BIH and an average CR of 10.87% on PTB. *(These figures are taken directly from the published abstract, since the full text was not accessible to us; the compression ratio is reported by the authors as a percentage rather than the more conventional dimensionless ratio used elsewhere in this review, and this discrepancy could not be resolved without the full text — please confirm the intended units before citing this figure.)* Collectively, these works demonstrate that FPGAs can support ECG compression algorithms, including adaptive-predictor and denoising-integrated designs, while maintaining real-time streaming capability.

## D. Summary and Research Gap

Most existing compression systems rely on a single algorithm applied uniformly across the ECG waveform, which limits their effectiveness across varying cardiac patterns. An adaptive compression framework capable of dynamically selecting compression strategies in response to signal characteristics holds clear advantages for jointly achieving high compression ratio and signal fidelity: during quiescent segments, a lightweight lossy scheme can maximize data reduction with minimal computational overhead, while lossless or near-lossless compression can be applied to morphologically complex, diagnostically important segments such as the QRS complex.

Hardware implementations of this kind of region-adaptive switching do exist in the literature reviewed here, and the strongest example — Tseng, Chen, and Lu's adaptive CS/NPC chip [30] — is close enough to this work's framing that the gap must be stated more precisely than in earlier drafts of this review. Their design switches between two fixed algorithms (compressed sensing and a near-lossless predictive/Huffman scheme) based on a *predetermined* partition of the R–R interval, computed from R–R timing rather than from any runtime assessment of local signal complexity; their target platform is a fixed-function ASIC, with the FPGA used only to prototype and demonstrate the design's resource usage rather than to provide field-reconfigurable switching. Similarly, the strongest software-level region-adaptive scheme reviewed here, Abo-Zahhad et al.'s DPCM/DWT hybrid [19], was evaluated only in an offline MATLAB environment with no real-time or hardware implementation.

The gap that remains open, therefore, is narrower than "no adaptive system has been implemented in real time" — such a system exists for one specific pair of algorithms, in an ASIC context. What has not been demonstrated in the literature reviewed here is an FPGA-based accelerator that dynamically selects among more than two compression strategies — spanning, for example, the time-domain, transform-domain, and lossless paradigms surveyed in Section B — based on real-time-assessed signal morphology rather than a fixed, precomputed temporal partition, while retaining the bounded-latency, reconfigurable-hardware streaming that FPGA platforms are shown in Section C to support. This work therefore proposes an FPGA-based compression accelerator that integrates real-time signal compression with reconfigurable cores, enabling resource-balanced parallel execution of adaptive, morphology-driven compression-strategy selection.
